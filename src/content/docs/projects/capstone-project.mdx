Our capstone team put a damper with electronically adjustable damping in a production mountain bike fork. The electromagnet did what we designed it to do, and you could feel the difference by hand. The whole prototype cost \$1400, which is \$200 to \$300 below the price of the two commercial forks we compared. It weighs 20 g more than the RockShox and 220 g more than the Fox. Getting there took two designs, because the first one barely responded to current.

The damper uses magnetorheological (MR) fluid, a carrier fluid with microscopic iron particles in it. With no magnetic field it flows like an ordinary fluid. With a field it becomes a Bingham plastic, which resists flow until the force passes a yield stress. A stronger field gives more damping force. Cars and seismic dampers already use this fluid.

## The first design failed

EMBR V1 kept as much of the stock fork as it could. It swapped in MR fluid and wrapped a coil around the outside of the pressure tube. On the dynamometer, 2, 3, and 4 A all gave nearly the same curve as no current at all.

<Figure name="damper/v1-dyno" number={2} alt="Force against velocity for no current, 2, 3, and 4 amps. All four curves nearly overlap.">
  EMBR V1 with MR fluid at increasing current, measured on a shock dynamometer. The axes keep the report's units, pounds-force (lbf) and inches per second (in/s), while the rest of this page uses newtons and meters per second. Critical design report, Fig. 12.
</Figure>

The team traced that to two causes. The compression circuit had too much open area, so the fluid never saw much restriction. And the field ran the wrong way. Iron particles line up along field lines, and shearing across that alignment takes much more force than shearing along it. At the orifice, V1's field ran parallel to the flow.

<Figure name="damper/v1-field" number={3} narrow plate={false} alt="Cross section of V1 with field lines looping around the outside coil and running along the tube at the center orifice.">
  How V1's field likely ran. At the center it lies along the flow. Critical design report, Fig. 13.
</Figure>

## The redesign

EMBR V2 moves the coil inside the pressure tube onto a steel piston, which channels the flux across the gap and perpendicular to the flow. There are no holes through the piston. All the fluid has to pass through a narrow annular (ring-shaped) gap between the piston and the tube.

<Figure name="damper/v2-cad" number={4} narrow alt="CAD section of the V2 damper showing the spring, the internal floating piston, and the coil on the steel piston at the bottom of the tube.">
  The V2 concept. Critical design report, Fig. 14.
</Figure>

### The flow model

The team modeled the gap as flow between two flat plates. That gives a pressure gradient, and the pressure drop pushes on the piston head:

$$
\frac{dP}{dy} = -\frac{12\,Q\,\mu}{a^3 W}, \qquad F = \left|\frac{dP}{dy}\right| L\, A_p
$$

Here $Q$ is the flow from the moving shaft, $\mu$ is the fluid viscosity, and $a$ is the gap. $W$ is the mean circumference, $L$ is the piston length, and $A_p$ is the piston area. The code isn't public because it was course or sponsor work.

```matlab title="Critical design report, appendix"
Q2 = vel.*A1;                                       % [m^3/s] volumetric flow rate
dPdy2 = -(12.*Q2.*mu_MR) ./ (a^3*W);
deltaP2 = abs(dPdy2).*L;                            % [Pa] pressure drop across
force_lbf = (deltaP2 .* A_p);                       % [N] force vs. velocity
```

The gap is cubed, so it dominates. V1's gap was 1.94 mm and V2's is 0.5 mm, which alone gives a factor of 58. The longer piston, larger area, and wider channel bring the total to 330 times the force, from 0.94 N to 310 N at 8 m/s.

## The electromagnet

The fluid only resists flow if the field crosses the gap perpendicular to the flow. That was the problem I worked on, with limits on space, power draw, and heat.

The coil is about 250 turns of 26 AWG copper on a 9 mm steel core. It runs at 2 A from a 40 V DC supply. I modeled it in Python by summing the field of each loop, since a coil this short does not match the infinite solenoid approximation. For one loop of radius $a$ carrying current $I$, at a point $(r, z)$:

$$
B_z = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(a+r)^2 + z^2}}\left[K + \frac{a^2 - r^2 - z^2}{(a-r)^2 + z^2}E\right]
$$

$K$ and $E$ are complete elliptic integrals. They have no closed form, and SciPy evaluates them directly. Adding the magnetized core gives an estimated peak of 0.15 T across the 2.5 mm radial gap modeled at the time. That number comes from the model, and the design report calls the geometry difficult to model. The steel parts carry the flux, so the field crosses the gap where the fluid flows.

## Control and heat

Two accelerometers track the motion of the uppers against the lowers, and an Arduino drives the electromagnet. It uses PWM (pulse-width modulation, switching quickly between on and off) to vary the coil current, and with it the field. Graham Adleman programmed the Arduino, and I helped him design the system. Control is needed for two reasons. Holding the field on during rebound would trap fluid and risk cavitation. And the coil turns 20 to 40 W into heat at full power, so it can only run when damping is needed. Curb tests at 15 mph with BMI160 accelerometers showed the pair could tell when a compression starts, when it peaks, and when rebound begins.

## How we checked it

### Flow model against CFD

The team compared a flow model of the gap against Ansys Fluent computational fluid dynamics (CFD) at three axle velocities. The difference shrinks as velocity rises. These numbers come from the poster and use an earlier piston geometry, so they are lower than the model in Figure 5. The forces are rounded to the newton, and the differences were computed before rounding.

| Axle velocity | Flow model | Fluent CFD | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 m/s | 20 N | 16 N | 16.5% |
| 4 m/s | 45 N | 40 N | 10.9% |
| 6 m/s | 80 N | 74 N | 7.2% |
