Our capstone team put a damper with electronically adjustable damping in a production mountain bike fork. The electromagnet did what we designed it to do, and the first test found a noticeable change in damping at each level of current. Getting there took two designs, because the first one barely responded to current.

The team estimated a retail price of about \$1400, which is \$200 to \$300 below the two commercial forks we compared. The prototype's parts cost \$975.59: \$415.59 the team bought, plus the \$560 fork and damper the client supplied. The finished fork weighs 20 g more than the RockShox and 220 g more than the Fox.

The damper uses magnetorheological (MR) fluid, a carrier fluid with microscopic iron particles in it. With no magnetic field it flows like an ordinary fluid. With a field it becomes a Bingham plastic, which does not flow until the stress on it passes a yield stress. A stronger field gives more damping force. Car suspensions and seismic dampers already use this fluid.

## The first design failed

EMBR V1 kept as much of the stock fork as it could. It swapped in MR fluid and wrapped a coil around the outside of the pressure tube. On the dynamometer, 2, 3, and 4 A all gave nearly the same curve as no current at all.

<Figure name="damper/v1-dyno" number={2} alt="Force against velocity for no current, 2, 3, and 4 amps. All four curves nearly overlap.">
  EMBR V1 with MR fluid at increasing current, measured on a shock dynamometer. The axes keep the report's units, pounds-force (lbf) and inches per second (in/s), while the rest of this page uses newtons and meters per second. Critical design report, Fig. 12.
</Figure>

The team traced that to two causes. The compression circuit had too much open area, so the fluid never saw much restriction. And the field ran the wrong way. Iron particles line up along field lines, and shearing across that alignment takes much more force than shearing along it. At the orifice, V1's field ran parallel to the flow.

<Figure name="damper/v1-field" number={3} narrow plate={false} alt="Cross section of V1 with field lines looping around the outside coil and running along the tube at the center orifice.">
  How V1's field likely ran. At the center it lies along the flow. Critical design report, Fig. 13.
</Figure>

## The redesign

EMBR V2 moves the coil inside the pressure tube onto a steel piston, which channels the flux across the gap and perpendicular to the flow. There are no holes through the piston. All the fluid has to pass through a narrow annular (ring-shaped) gap between the piston and the tube.

<Figure name="damper/v2-cad" number={4} narrow alt="CAD section of the V2 damper showing the spring, the internal floating piston, and the coil on the steel piston at the bottom of the tube.">

</Figure>

### The flow model

The team modeled the flow through the gap as flow between two flat plates. That gives a pressure gradient, and the pressure drop pushes on the piston head:

$$
\frac{dP}{dy} = -\frac{12\,Q\,\mu}{a^3 W}, \qquad F = \left|\frac{dP}{dy}\right| L\, A_p
$$

Here $Q$ is the flow from the moving shaft, $\mu$ is the fluid viscosity, and $a$ is the gap. $W$ is the mean circumference, $L$ is the piston length, and $A_p$ is the piston area. The code isn't public because it was course or sponsor work.

```matlab title="Critical design report, appendix"
Q2 = vel.*A1;                                       % [m^3/s] volumetric flow rate
dPdy2 = -(12.*Q2.*mu_MR) ./ (a^3*W);
deltaP2 = abs(dPdy2).*L;                            % [Pa] pressure drop across
force_lbf = (deltaP2 .* A_p);                       % [N] force vs. velocity
```

The gap is cubed, so it dominates. V1's gap was 1.94 mm and the V2 concept's is 0.5 mm, which alone gives a factor of 58. The longer piston and larger area more than make up for the slightly wider channel, and bring the total to 330 times the force, from 0.94 N to 310 N at 8 m/s.

<DamperFigure number={5} />

The final build used a wider gap. The team ran the same flow model and CFD for 18, 18.5, and 19 mm pistons and found that damping climbs steeply as the piston nears the tube wall. They chose an 18.5 mm steel piston, the least damping with the coil off that still kept the gap small, and checked the trend by cycling the damper by hand with 3D-printed mock pistons.

## The electromagnet

The fluid resists flow most when the field crosses the gap perpendicular to the flow. Getting the field to cross the gap that way, within limits on space, power draw, and heat, was my part of the project.

The final coil is 160 turns of 26 AWG copper on a 9 mm steel core, run at about 2 A from an 8 V supply. I first modeled it in Python by summing the field of each loop, since the infinite solenoid approximation does not hold for a coil this short. For one loop of radius $a$ carrying current $I$, at a point $(r, z)$:

$$
B_z = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(a+r)^2 + z^2}}\left[K + \frac{a^2 - r^2 - z^2}{(a-r)^2 + z^2}E\right]
$$

$K$ and $E$ are complete elliptic integrals. They have no closed form, and SciPy evaluates them directly. Adding the magnetized core gives an estimated peak of 0.15 T across the 2.5 mm radial gap modeled at the time. That number comes from the model, and the critical design report calls the geometry difficult to model. For the final design, a 2D nonlinear finite element model in MATLAB's PDE Toolbox traced the flux through the steel parts. A steel sleeve around the outside of the tube completes the magnetic circuit, so the field crosses the gap where the fluid flows.

## Control and heat

Two accelerometers track how the fork's upper and lower halves move against each other, and an Arduino drives the electromagnet. It uses PWM (pulse-width modulation, switching quickly between on and off) to vary the coil current, and with it the field. Graham Adleman programmed the Arduino, and I helped him design the system. Control is needed for two reasons. Holding the field on during rebound would trap fluid and risk cavitation. And the coil is effectively a resistive heater, so running it only when damping is needed limits the heat it adds to the damper and saves battery. Curb tests at 15 mph with BMI160 accelerometers showed the pair could tell when a compression starts, when it peaks, and when rebound begins.

## How we checked it

### Flow model against CFD

The team compared the flow model against Ansys Fluent computational fluid dynamics (CFD) for the final 18.5 mm piston at four axle velocities. The difference shrinks as velocity rises. The final piston's wider gap gives much lower forces than the V2 concept in Figure 5. Final design report, Table 1.

| Axle velocity | Flow model | Fluent CFD | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 m/s | 8.42 N | 6.71 N | 20.3% |
| 2 m/s | 18.75 N | 15.65 N | 16.5% |
| 4 m/s | 45.15 N | 40.21 N | 10.9% |
| 6 m/s | 79.18 N | 73.48 N | 7.21% |

### Dynamometer

A shock dynamometer then measured the finished damper, and the results were inconclusive. The school's dyno reaches only 14 in/s, while mountain bike dampers are usually tested to about 80 in/s, so only low-speed damping could be measured. On constant power from a power supply, the damper cavitated. With the control system wired in, its early algorithm could not pick out compression and rebound on the dyno. The final numbers can't be made public.
