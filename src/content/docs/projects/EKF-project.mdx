The unscented filter pulls ahead when the fluid's yield force gets large. With a larger yield constant and a wider sampling spread, it cut the force error from 625 N to 401 N. It also kept every sample inside its bounds. At the report's parameters the two filters agree to within 0.4 N.

A Kalman filter estimates values a sensor cannot read by combining a model with noisy measurements. This was my final project for EENG 519. It ran in MATLAB and Simulink, and nothing here ran on the bike.

The [EMBR damper](/projects/capstone-project/) changes its damping by changing the yield stress of a magnetorheological (MR) fluid. Low-cost sensors cannot measure the field intensity or the force coming up from the trail. So the filters infer them from a string potentiometer and accelerometers.

## The model

The suspension is a sprung mass with displacement $z$. An unmeasured force $F_{in}$ drives it, and the spring and damper resist:

$$
m\ddot{z} = F_{in} - kz - K_v\dot{z} - K_m h \tanh(\alpha\dot{z})
$$

Field intensity $h$ follows the coil current $i$ with a first-order lag:

$$
\dot{h} = -\frac{1}{\tau}h + \frac{1}{\tau}i
$$

The $\tanh(\alpha\dot{z})$ term models the fluid's yield transition. With $\alpha = 100$ s/m it changes quickly around zero velocity.

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Equivalent mass $m$ | 100 kg |
| Spring rate $k$ | 10000 N/m |
| Viscous coefficient $K_v$ | 1000 N·s/m |
| Magnetic yield constant $K_m$ | 0.005 |
| Transition sharpness $\alpha$ | 100 s/m |
| Field time constant $\tau$ | 0.05 s |
| Sample time $T_s$ | 0.01 s |

The simulated trail combines 1.5 Hz roughness, 8 Hz chatter, Gaussian surface noise, and two obstacles at $t = 1$ s and $t = 2$ s. Coil current sits at 1 A during chatter and steps to 2 A when obstacle force passes 750 N.

```matlab title="Main.m"
F_harmonic = ...
    100 * sin(2*pi*f1*t) + ...  % Primary undulating terrain

F_pulse1    = pulse1_amp * exp(-((t - pulse1_mu).^2) / (2 * pulse1_sig^2));

```

## Estimating the terrain force

I wanted the filters to estimate the terrain force directly, so I modeled it as a discrete random walk and added it to the state:

$$
F_{in,k+1} = F_{in,k} + s_k, \qquad \tilde{x}_k = [z_k,\ \dot{z}_k,\ h_k,\ F_{in,k}]^T
$$

Here $s_k$ is zero-mean Gaussian noise with variance $Q_s = 5 \times 10^6\ \text{N}^2$. An Euler step discretizes the dynamics. The filters measure displacement and acceleration.

## Extended Kalman filter

The extended Kalman filter (EKF) linearizes the dynamics at each step. It propagates covariance, its estimate of its own uncertainty, through the resulting Jacobians (matrices of partial derivatives). Two entries carry the nonlinearity:

```matlab title="Main.m"
da_dzd  = -(Kv/m) - (Km/m)*h_hat*alpha*(sech(alpha*zd_hat))^2;
da_dh   = -(Km/m)*tanh(alpha*zd_hat);
```

The EKF tracked displacement, velocity, and the low-frequency terrain, and its errors stayed inside the $\pm 3\sigma$ bounds (three standard deviations) for the whole 3 s run. The force estimate lags slightly, and the report attributes that to the 8 Hz chatter.

## Unscented Kalman filter

The unscented Kalman filter (UKF) skips the Jacobians. It passes a small set of chosen sample states, called sigma points, through the nonlinear functions directly. It has 9 sigma points ($2L + 1$ with $L = 4$) and uses the same $Q$ and $R$ as the EKF.

```matlab title="Main.m"
alpha_ukf = 1e-3;   % Spread of sigma points
ki        = 0;      % Secondary scaling parameter
beta      = 2;      % Optimal for Gaussian distributions
lambda    = alpha_ukf^2 * (L + ki) - L;
c         = L + lambda;

Wm = [lambda/c, 0.5/c * ones(1, 2*L)];
```

## Why the two filters agree

At these numbers the nonlinear term is tiny. Since $|\tanh| \le 1$ and $h$ peaks near 2,

$$
|K_m h \tanh(\alpha\dot{z})| \le 0.005 \times 2 = 0.01\ \text{N}
$$

The viscous force at the same moment is $K_v|\dot{z}|$, or hundreds of newtons. So the plant is close to linear, and both filters reduce to the same linear Kalman filter. It also means the coil current has almost no effect on the motion in this simulation.

The chosen sigma point spread matters too. With $\kappa = 0$, the code above spreads the points by

$$
\sqrt{L + \lambda} = \alpha_{ukf}\sqrt{L} = 0.002
$$

and gives the center point a weight of

$$
W_0 = \frac{\lambda}{L + \lambda} = 1 - \frac{1}{\alpha_{ukf}^2} = -999{,}999
$$

The points sit within 0.002 standard deviations of the mean, so they sample the curve about as locally as a Jacobian does. They also never see the width of the tanh step.

## Raising the yield constant

Raising $K_m$ makes that term matter. Over eight noise seeds at $K_m = 500$, where the yield force reaches 1000 N, the three configurations separate:

| Filter at $K_m = 500$ | RMS error | Samples inside $\pm 3\sigma$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EKF | 625 N | 58% |
| UKF, $\alpha = 0.001$ | 675 N | 74% |
| UKF, $\alpha = 1$ | 401 N | 100% |

The EKF becomes overconfident, so its errors leave its bounds. The UKF with a wide spread stays inside its bounds. The UKF with the report's spread does no better than the EKF on error. So the report's argument for the UKF holds, but only with a larger $K_m$ and a wider $\alpha_{ukf}$ than it used.

<EstimationFigure number={5} />

Fitting $K_m$ to dynamometer data would settle which of these cases describes the real fork. The report's stated next step is to run the UKF on a microcontroller and measure its cost against the EKF.

If I did this again, I would tune the filters for biking. I tuned them for the simulation and simple inputs, and I want more randomness in those inputs and more tuning against them.
